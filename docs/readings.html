Readings

Weekly readings, presentation candidates, and the Slack rhythm for discussion posts.

Game plan for the semester: how readings, Slack signups, and discussion posts fit together — see the readings & discussion game plan.

Weekly reading list

Week 1 · 2026-04-17 — Intro + Basic Bayes

Required

  • Austerweil, J. A Narrative Introduction to Probability — Tutorial 1 Ch 1–3 (Goals, Hungry Bayes, Probability by Counting).
    Post-class reinforcement of Week 1's probability foundations.
  • Austerweil, J. A Narrative Introduction to Probability — Tutorial 2 Ch 0–1 (Getting Started, Python Basics).
    Sets up GenJAX on Colab so students can hit the ground running in Week 2.

Week 2 · 2026-04-24 — Levels of analysis + Bayes continued

Required

  • Austerweil, J. A Narrative Introduction to Probability — Tutorial 1 Ch 4–5 (Conditional probability, Bayes).
    Consolidates Week 1 foundations with the Chibany/bento framing used in Week 2's worked example.
  • Austerweil, J. A Narrative Introduction to Probability — Tutorial 3 Ch 1 (Chibany's Mystery Bentos).
    Introduces the bento scenario used in Week 2's Marr-levels walkthrough.

Optional

  • Marr, D. (1982). Vision, Ch. 1: The Philosophy and the Approach. San Francisco: W. H. Freeman.
    Founding statement of the three levels of analysis. Not required because no PDF available; read if interested in the framing.

Week 3 · 2026-05-15 — Conjugate Bayes + topic models

Required

  • Austerweil, J. A Narrative Introduction to Probability — Tutorial 3 Ch 4 (Bayesian Learning with Gaussians).
    Picks up exactly where Week 2's Block 7 left off — conjugate Gaussian updates explained in full.
  • Austerweil, J. A Narrative Introduction to Probability — Tutorial 1 Ch 6 (Glossary review).
    One-stop reference for the notation and terms Week 3 assumes.

Optional

  • Griffiths, T. L., & Tenenbaum, J. B. (2001). Randomness and coincidences: Reconciling intuition and probability theory. Proc. CogSci.
    SP25 Week 3 companion: how people judge randomness — a nice bridge from conjugate Bayes into cognitive modeling.

Week 4 · 2026-05-22 — Bayesian generalization + hierarchical Bayes

Required

  • Tenenbaum, J. B., & Griffiths, T. L. (2001). Generalization, similarity, and Bayesian inference. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 24(4), 629–640.
    The canonical Bayesian-generalization paper. This week's math is a worked example of this paper.

Optional

  • Austerweil, J. A Narrative Introduction to Probability — Tutorial 3 Ch 5 (Mixture models).
    Reading for pre-class: hierarchical Bayes intuition before the lecture.

Presentation candidates

Not yet assigned. Rank your top 3 in Slack #paper-signups (see the game plan).

  • Tenenbaum, J. B., & Xu, F. (2000). Word learning as Bayesian inference. Proc. CogSci.
    Classic application of Bayesian generalization to word learning.
  • Kemp, C., & Tenenbaum, J. B. (2009). Structured statistical models of inductive reasoning. Psychological Review, 116(1), 20–58.
    Hierarchical Bayes for inductive reasoning — direct extension of the lecture.
  • Hornsby, A. N., Evans, T., Riefer, P. S., Prior, R., & Love, B. C. (2020). Conceptual organization is revealed by consumer activity patterns. Computational Brain & Behavior, 3, 162–173.
    Recent extension of Bayesian categorization into real consumer data.

Week 5 · 2026-05-29 — Bayes nets + causal Bayes nets

Required

  • Austerweil, J. A Narrative Introduction to Probability — Tutorial 3 Ch 5 (Mixture models), Ch 6 (DPMM).
    Finishes the hierarchical-Bayes thread from Week 4 before we move to graphical models.

Presentation candidates

Not yet assigned. Rank your top 3 in Slack #paper-signups (see the game plan).

  • Gerstenberg, T., Goodman, N. D., Lagnado, D. A., & Tenenbaum, J. B. (2015). How, whether, why: Causal judgments as counterfactual contrasts. Proc. CogSci.
    Causal reasoning via counterfactuals — frames how people use causal Bayes nets.
  • Lagnado, D. A., & Sloman, S. (2002). Learning causal structure. Proc. CogSci.
    Foundational paper on people learning causal Bayes net structure from interventions.
  • Tenenbaum, J. B., Kemp, C., Griffiths, T. L., & Goodman, N. D. (2011). How to grow a mind: statistics, structure, and abstraction. Science, 331(6022), 1279–1285.
    The 'grow a mind' review — connects Bayes nets to hierarchical Bayesian cognition.

Week 6 · 2026-06-05 — Markov chains + networks

Required

  • Abbott, J. T., Austerweil, J. L., & Griffiths, T. L. (2012). Human memory search as a random walk in a semantic network. NeurIPS.
    Memory retrieval modeled as a random walk — concrete cognitive application of Markov chains on networks.

Presentation candidates

Not yet assigned. Rank your top 3 in Slack #paper-signups (see the game plan).

  • Steyvers, M., & Tenenbaum, J. B. (2005). The large-scale structure of semantic networks: Statistical analyses and a model of semantic growth. Cognitive Science, 29(1), 41–78.
    Small-world structure in semantic networks — sets the network for Abbott et al. 2012.
  • Zemla, J. C., & Austerweil, J. L. (2018). Estimating semantic networks of groups and individuals from fluency data. Computational Brain & Behavior, 1, 36–58.
    Extends Abbott 2012 to estimating individual vs. group networks.
  • Stella, M. (2018). Cohort and rhyme priming emerge from the multiplex network structure of the mental lexicon. Complexity.
    Modern multiplex-network model of mental lexicon priming.

Week 7 · 2026-06-12 — Monte Carlo approximation

Required

  • Vul, E., Goodman, N. D., Griffiths, T. L., & Tenenbaum, J. B. (2009). One and done? Optimal decisions from very few samples. Proc. CogSci.
    Why people may be rational *samplers* even when they look irrational — direct application of MC.

Presentation candidates

Not yet assigned. Rank your top 3 in Slack #paper-signups (see the game plan).

  • Sanborn, A. N., & Griffiths, T. L. (2008). Markov chain Monte Carlo with people. NIPS.
    MCMC-with-people as a method for measuring perceptual categories.
  • Griffiths, T. L., & Tenenbaum, J. B. (2006). Optimal predictions in everyday cognition. Psychological Science, 17(9), 767–773.
    People's predictions match Bayesian posteriors — the paper that argued we're MC samplers.
  • de Zoete, J., Sjerps, M., & Morrison, G. S. (2019). Cross-lingual comparison of acoustic features for forensic voice comparison. Speech Communication.
    Forensic application of MC-style Bayesian evidence evaluation.

Week 8 · 2026-06-19 — Statistical decision theory, MDPs, and reinforcement learning

Required

  • Daw, N. D., Niv, Y., & Dayan, P. (2005). Uncertainty-based competition between prefrontal and dorsolateral striatal systems for behavioral control. Nature Neuroscience, 8(12), 1704–1711.
    Canonical dual-systems RL paper — model-based vs. model-free.

Presentation candidates

Not yet assigned. Rank your top 3 in Slack #paper-signups (see the game plan).

  • Schultz, W., Dayan, P., & Montague, P. R. (1997). A neural substrate of prediction and reward. Science, 275(5306), 1593–1599.
    The dopamine-as-TD-error paper — foundational for neuroscience of RL.
  • Körding, K. P., & Wolpert, D. M. (2004). Bayesian integration in sensorimotor learning. Nature, 427(6971), 244–247.
    SDT applied to motor control — perception-action Bayesian integration.
  • Sims, C. R. (2018). Efficient coding explains the universal law of generalization in human perception. Science, 360(6389), 652–656.
    Information-theoretic bound on perception — SDT with a capacity constraint.

Week 9 · 2026-06-26 — Inverse RL + social cognition

Required

  • Baker, C. L., Saxe, R., & Tenenbaum, J. B. (2009). Action understanding as inverse planning. Cognition, 113(3), 329–349.
    Foundational IRL-for-social-cognition paper.

Presentation candidates

Not yet assigned. Rank your top 3 in Slack #paper-signups (see the game plan).

  • Ho, M. K., Littman, M. L., MacGlashan, J., Cushman, F., & Austerweil, J. L. (2016). Showing versus doing: Teaching by demonstration. NeurIPS.
    Teaching-by-demonstration as inverse planning — Prof. Austerweil's own work.
  • Ho, M. K., Littman, M. L., Cushman, F., & Austerweil, J. L. (2015). Teaching with rewards and punishments: Reinforcement or communication? Proc. CogSci.
    Follow-up: reward as signal vs. as reinforcer.
  • Ho, M. K., Cushman, F., Littman, M. L., & Austerweil, J. L. (2017). Social is special: A normative framework for teaching with and learning from evaluative feedback. Cognition.
    Normative framework for teaching — rounds out the Ho trilogy.
  • Jara-Ettinger, J. (2019). Theory of mind as inverse reinforcement learning. Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences, 29, 105–110.
    Recent review paper framing ToM explicitly as IRL.

Week 10 · 2026-07-03 — Bayesian nonparametrics

Required

  • Austerweil, J. L., Gershman, S. J., Tenenbaum, J. B., & Griffiths, T. L. (2015). Structure and flexibility in Bayesian models of cognition. Oxford Handbook chapter.
    Intuitive introduction to Bayesian nonparametrics in cognition — by the instructor.

Presentation candidates

Not yet assigned. Rank your top 3 in Slack #paper-signups (see the game plan).

  • Griffiths, T. L., & Ghahramani, Z. (2011). The Indian Buffet Process: An introduction and review. JMLR.
    Canonical IBP tutorial — best starting point for feature-learning BNP.
  • Austerweil, J. L., & Griffiths, T. L. (2013). A nonparametric Bayesian framework for constructing flexible feature representations. Psychological Review, 120(4), 817.
    IBP applied to feature learning — instructor's own work.
  • Gershman, S. J., & Blei, D. M. (2012). A tutorial on Bayesian nonparametric models. Journal of Mathematical Psychology, 56(1), 1–12.
    Accessible tutorial review.

Week 11 · 2026-07-10 — Deep neural networks + large language models

Required

  • Lake, B. M., Ullman, T. D., Tenenbaum, J. B., & Gershman, S. J. (2017). Building machines that learn and think like people. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 40, e253.
    The 'what's missing from deep learning' position paper — use as the opening frame for NNs vs. structured cognition.

Presentation candidates

Not yet assigned. Rank your top 3 in Slack #paper-signups (see the game plan).

  • Mikolov, T., Chen, K., Corrado, G., & Dean, J. (2013). Efficient estimation of word representations in vector space. arXiv:1301.3781.
    Classic word2vec paper — the foundation of modern LLM embeddings.
  • Pereira, F., Lou, B., Pritchett, B., Ritter, S., Gershman, S. J., Kanwisher, N., Botvinick, M., & Fedorenko, E. (2018). Toward a universal decoder of linguistic meaning from brain activation. Nature Communications, 9, 963.
    Decoding meaning from fMRI with embeddings — bridges NNs and cognition.
  • Shulze-Buschoff, L., Akata, Z., Bethge, M., & Schrimpf, M. (2025). Visual cognition in multimodal large language models.
    Very recent (2025) probe of what visual cognition modern multimodal LLMs actually do.
  • Costello, T. H., Pennycook, G., & Rand, D. G. (2024). Durably reducing conspiracy beliefs through dialogues with AI. Science.
    2024 study on LLMs as persuasive agents — modern case for what deep models DO in the wild.

Week 12 · 2026-07-17 — Ethics, adversarial ML, and project presentations

Required

  • Caliskan, A., Bryson, J. J., & Narayanan, A. (2017). Semantics derived automatically from language corpora contain human-like biases. Science, 356(6334), 183–186.
    Foundational paper showing that word embeddings inherit human bias — still urgently relevant.

Presentation candidates

Not yet assigned. Rank your top 3 in Slack #paper-signups (see the game plan).

  • Buolamwini, J., & Gebru, T. (2018). Gender shades: Intersectional accuracy disparities in commercial gender classification. PMLR.
    Canonical paper on disparate accuracy in computer vision systems.
  • Ensign, D., Friedler, S. A., Neville, S., Scheidegger, C., & Venkatasubramanian, S. (2018). Runaway feedback loops in predictive policing. PMLR.
    Predictive-policing feedback loops — concrete example of ML deployment harm.
  • Rahwan, I., Cebrian, M., Obradovich, N. et al. (2019). Machine behaviour. Nature, 568(7753), 477–486.
    Nature review framing study of machine behavior as its own discipline.
  • Miller, E. J., Foo, Y. Z., Mewton, P., & Dawel, A. (2023). AI hyperrealism: Why AI faces are perceived as more real than human ones. Psychological Science.
    2023 paper on uncanny-valley inversion — modern ethical concern around AI-generated faces.
